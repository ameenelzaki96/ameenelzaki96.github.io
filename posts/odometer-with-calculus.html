Checking a vehicle's odometer with first-year calculus

17 Nov 2026·6 min read·Fleet

Every tracking platform reports a trip distance. But which distance? A modern telematics device gives you at least three, and they rarely agree. I took one short trip, about nine minutes in Riyadh, and computed it three ways.

Distance is the area under the speed curve

If you plot speed against time, the area under the curve is the distance travelled. That's the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to a car. The device samples speed every few seconds, so I approximated the integral with the trapezoidal rule: join consecutive points with straight lines and add up the trapezoids.

distance ≈ Σ ½ · (v[i] + v[i+1]) · (t[i+1] − t[i])

For speed I used GPS speed (position_speed). The device also reports CAN-bus speed, and for this trip the two were nearly identical.

Three answers

MethodDistance
Integral of speed (trapezoidal)3.763 km
GPS distance (sum of point-to-point)3.858 km
CAN odometer (end − start)4.4 km

Why they disagree, and why that's fine

  • The integral runs slightly low because straight lines between samples cut corners whenever the car accelerates or brakes between readings. Sparse samples mean more lost area.
  • GPS distance runs slightly high because position jitter adds small zig-zags, and each one adds a few metres.
  • The CAN odometer usually reports in 0.1 km or whole-km steps, so on a 4 km trip the rounding alone can cause a large relative error. Over a month of driving it is the most reliable of the three.
Two independent methods agreeing within 3% is a strong check that the data is sound.

The practical use is validation. When a customer disputes a trip distance, or a device looks miscalibrated, an independent calculation from raw speed is a quick, explainable check. If the integral and the GPS distance agree but the odometer is far off over a long period, the odometer reading is probably the problem.

Writing it in Arabic

I built the report in Arabic, right-to-left, because the people using it work in Arabic. Getting charts, numbers and equations to sit correctly in an RTL page takes more care than people expect, but it makes the difference between a report people read and one they skip.
