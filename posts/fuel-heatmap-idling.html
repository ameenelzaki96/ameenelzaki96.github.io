The fuel wasn't on the highway. It was in the parking lot.

27 Aug 2026·6 min read·Fleet

A logistics operator wanted to know which truck burned the most fuel and why. I expected the answer to be about routes: the long Riyadh–Jeddah run, maybe a detour or two. The data said otherwise.

Step one: find the top consumer

"Most fuel" means the most litres actually filled, not an estimate. One heavy truck stood out: 3,197 L over 32,526 km between April and August, with 24 Riyadh⇄Jeddah legs (about 12 round trips) and 173 hours of idling.

Seen on its own, that looks bad. It looks less bad once you compare it with the rest of the fleet.

Step two: put the whole fleet on one map

I aggregated GPS positions onto a ~110 m grid and weighted each cell by dwell density, so the heat map shows where trucks spend time rather than just where they pass through. Then I reduced each truck's timeline to a sequence of city visits and counted every crossing, which made the corridors visible:

  • Riyadh ⇄ Jeddah dominates, with 37 legs
  • followed by Riyadh ⇄ Makkah and Jeddah ⇄ Makkah

Step three: the surprise

The fleet turned out to be a two-tier operation. Heavy trucks run the corridor at about 9.7 L/100 km with very little idling. Light pickups handle local Riyadh work, and they idle far more per kilometre driven.

The obvious fix, moving city trucks onto the corridor, wouldn't save anything.

The two roles need different weight classes, and the waste isn't in the long-haul runs. It's in engines left running at drop-offs, in traffic and in yards. The levers that matter are:

  1. Cut idling in the city fleet, through driver feedback, idle alerts and engine-off policies.
  2. Maximize load on corridor runs, because a half-empty heavy truck on a 950 km leg is where real money goes.

What I took away

The person asking usually has a hypothesis built into the question. "Which route costs the most?" assumes the problem is a route. A heat map weighted by time spent rather than distance covered reframed the problem around behaviour.

The map was also anonymized: no plates, no company names, coordinates coarsened to about 110 m, so no depot or customer stop can be pinpointed.
